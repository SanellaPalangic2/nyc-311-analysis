Tests whether the 8 survival curves are all the same. Chi-square = 3,088,648.61, df = 7, p < 1e-300 (underflows to 0), n = 3,524,524. The curves differ, but with millions of rows any difference is significant, so the size of the gaps (table above) matters more than the p-value.

### Cox proportional hazards model

Fit on a random sample of 250,000 requests with a known borough (seed 42; 246,652 closed in the sample), because fitting all 3.6M rows is slow and memory-heavy in lifelines. Covariates: agency, borough, season, and the top 10 complaint types by volume. Reference groups: NYPD, Brooklyn, Winter, and complaint types outside the top 10. Agencies with fewer than 5,000 requests in 2025 (DOE, OOS, OTI) share one "small agencies" group: alone they'd have too few requests in the sample for a stable estimate.

Hazard ratio above 1 = closes faster than the reference at any given moment; below 1 = slower. Complaint-type ratios are relative to other types *within the same agency*, since agency is also in the model.

- Concordance index: 0.7704 (0.5 = random ordering, 1 = perfect)
- Stability: refitting on a second independent sample (seed 43) changed log hazard ratios by at most 0.0766 (agency: small agencies (DOE, OOS, OTI)).

### Does the proportional hazards assumption hold?

Test (scaled Schoenfeld residuals, rank time transform), run on a separate 50,000-row fit with the same covariates (seed 42), because its run time grows with roughly the square of the sample size: **22 of 29 covariates violate it at p < 0.05.**

In plain terms: the Cox model assumes each group's closing rate is a *fixed multiple* of the reference group's, at every point in time. That isn't true here: the ratio between groups changes over time (for example, an agency whose median is about an hour does most of its closing early, while agencies with medians of days or weeks close later). `1d_ph_check_loglog.png` shows this visually: under the assumption, the agency curves would run parallel.

What it means: each hazard ratio is best read as an **average effect over the follow-up period**, not a constant one, and its direction and rough size are more trustworthy than its exact value. The Kaplan-Meier medians and curves don't rely on this assumption, so they're the main result. (With 50,000 rows the test detects even tiny violations, so the visual check matters as much as the p-values.) A stratified Cox model or time-varying effects would be the next step.

## Stage 2: Statistical testing

Script: `analysis/stats_tests.py`. Uses the 3,599,434 closed requests (the same rows as the earlier medians), because these tests need an observed resolution time; open requests are handled by the survival analysis in Stage 1. Borough tests use the 3,597,064 requests with a known borough.

### Kruskal-Wallis tests

Kruskal-Wallis asks whether the groups' resolution times come from the same distribution. It compares ranks, not raw hours, so it doesn't assume normally distributed data (resolution times are extremely skewed) and isn't dominated by the few year-long requests.

| Factor | Groups | Requests | H | df | p | Epsilon-squared |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| agency | 15 | 3,599,434 | 1,993,176.78 | 14 | < 1e-300 (underflows to 0) | 0.553747 |
| borough | 5 | 3,597,064 | 17,489.94 | 4 | < 1e-300 (underflows to 0) | 0.004862 |
| season | 4 | 3,599,434 | 23,790.96 | 3 | < 1e-300 (underflows to 0) | 0.006610 |

**Why the effect size matters more than the p-value here.** A p-value answers "could a difference this large be chance?" With millions of rows, even a difference of a few minutes is not chance, so every p-value is essentially 0 and they can't be used to rank the factors. Epsilon-squared answers a different question: what share of the variation in (ranked) resolution time does the grouping explain, from 0 (none) to 1 (all)? That's the measure of *how much* each factor matters.

**Does this support the finding that agency matters more than borough or season?** Yes: agency explains 55.37% of the rank variation in resolution time (epsilon-squared 0.553747), 83.8 times season's (0.006610), 113.9 times borough's (0.004862).

## Stage 3: Prediction model

Script: `analysis/prediction.py`. Target: **closed within 7 days** of being created (1 = yes). Still-open requests count as "no" (they've been open 9+ months); 6,940 requests whose closing time is unknown are left out.

- **Features (known at creation only):** agency, complaint_type, borough, channel, month, day_of_week, hour. Nothing from the close date, status or resolution text is used (the script refuses to run if one of those columns becomes a feature).
- **Time-based split:** train on Jan-Sep 2025 (created) (2,674,661 requests), test on Oct-Dec 2025 (created) (973,441 requests). This mimics real use: learn from the past, predict the future.
- **Class balance:** 83.71% of test requests closed within 7 days (82.67% in training). So a model that always says "yes" already looks good on the "yes" class; the **slow** class (not closed within 7 days) is where the models have to prove themselves, so metrics are shown for both.
- **Threshold:** a request is predicted "within 7 days" when its predicted probability is at least 0.5.

**Best model: Gradient boosting**, ROC AUC 0.9556 vs 0.5000 for the majority-class baseline. On the slow class it reaches precision 0.7033 and recall 0.6407 (F1 0.6705); the baseline never predicts a slow request, so its slow-class scores are 0. Logistic regression is close behind (AUC 0.9544, 0.0012 lower) and at the 0.5 threshold it actually scores higher on the slow class (F1 0.7139 vs 0.6705: recall 0.7680 vs 0.6407). AUC measures ranking across *all* thresholds, F1 only at one, so the threshold should be chosen for the use: lower it to catch more slow requests at the cost of more false alarms.

**Why the AUC is so high:** complaint type and agency are known when a request is filed and largely decide which process handles it (Stage 2: agency alone explains over half of the variation in resolution time). This isn't leakage: no information from after the request was created is used.

### Calibration of the best model

Brier score 0.0622 (mean squared error of the predicted probabilities; lower is better). The test set was split into 10 bins with equal numbers of requests; 6 of them have a mean predicted probability above 0.98 (most requests are predicted to be near-certain to close within 7 days), so those points overlap on the chart. Predicted and actual shares differ by at most 0.0824 (mean 0.0142); the bins with the largest gaps are where the probabilities shouldn't be taken at face value.

### Permutation feature importance

Each feature is shuffled in 200,000 random test rows (5 times, seed 42) and the drop in ROC AUC is measured: the bigger the drop, the more the model relies on it. Agency and complaint type are also shuffled together, because they overlap heavily (most complaint types belong to a single agency).

| Feature | AUC drop | Std. dev. |
|---|---:|---:|
| agency + complaint_type | 0.4597 | 0.0009 |
| complaint_type | 0.2755 | 0.0010 |
| agency | 0.0671 | 0.0004 |
| borough | 0.0043 | 0.0001 |
| hour | 0.0004 | 0.0000 |
| channel | 0.0004 | 0.0000 |
| day_of_week | 0.0003 | 0.0000 |
| month | 0.0000 | 0.0000 |

**Does it confirm agency as the top driver?** Not on its own: complaint_type is the most important single feature (AUC drop 0.2755) and agency ranks #2 (0.0671). That's mostly overlap, not disagreement: complaint type nearly determines the agency, so when agency alone is shuffled the model still gets that information from complaint type. Shuffled together, agency + complaint type drop AUC by 0.4597, more than any single feature, so *who handles the request and what it's about* is the main driver.

**About month:** the model is trained on months 1-9 and tested on 10-12, so it never sees a test month during training. Month can only act as a trend that's extrapolated forward, and shuffling it within Oct-Dec (AUC drop 0.0000) measures little. A model trained on several years would be needed to learn real month effects.

## Limitations

- **Censoring assumption.** Kaplan-Meier and Cox assume a request that's still open is no different from others that have been open as long (non-informative censoring). If agencies leave certain hard cases open on purpose, the survival medians are still biased. 6,940 requests with an unknown closing time are left out entirely.
- **Proportional hazards fails** for 22 of 29 covariates, so the Cox hazard ratios are averages over time, not constant effects. The Cox model is fit on a 250,000-row sample and the assumption test on a smaller one; a second sample was used to check stability.
- **Closing time is an administrative record.** "Closed" means closed in the system, not necessarily fixed, and some agencies appear to close certain request types on a fixed schedule (e.g. near exactly 60 days), which shapes every time-based result.
- **Stage 2 uses closed requests only.** Kruskal-Wallis and the bootstrap need an observed time; the bootstrap CIs reflect sampling noise only, not data-quality problems, which is why they're so narrow.
- **Agency and complaint type overlap.** Most complaint types belong to one agency, so their separate effects (in the Cox model, the effect sizes and the feature importance) can't be cleanly split apart.
- **The prediction model covers one year.** It's tested only on Oct-Dec 2025, can't learn month effects (test months never appear in training), uses a fixed 0.5 threshold, and its permutation importance is measured on a 200,000-row sample of the test set.
- **Descriptive, not causal.** None of these methods show *why* an agency is slow; staffing, workflows and how each agency closes requests aren't in the data.
- **One snapshot of a live dataset.** Requests keep closing after the snapshot date, so rerunning the download later will shift the numbers slightly.
